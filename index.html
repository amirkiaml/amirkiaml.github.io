SMS Outreach & SDR Agent System

Three agents draft every cold message in parallel and a picker agent selects one against explicit criteria — no single model settling for its first attempt. Replies go to a stateful SDR agent, and the rules that carry legal weight are enforced in code, not prompts.

What it does, end to end

A lead scraper pulls home-service businesses from Google Places into Supabase, and a hook agent reads each business profile to find a specific angle worth opening on — that hook is what the three drafters compete over. Once the picker has chosen, the draft passes through code-level checks — compliance footer, GSM-7 sanitisation, suppression list, line-type guard — before Twilio sends it.

When a reply lands on the FastAPI webhook, a triage agent classifies it as interested, objection, not-now, or opt-out. Opt-outs are handled in code and never reach a model. Everything else goes to a stateful SDR agent that holds the conversation, grounded in a YAML knowledge base, and hands off to me the moment it hits something outside its scope. An operator console shows every thread with its full trace, and I can take over any conversation mid-flight.

The two agents that only exist to say no

Two guardrails wrap the reply path. A cheap keyword scope check runs first and catches the obvious out-of-bounds cases for nothing; an LLM classifier catches the rest. A separate grounding judge checks that every claim in a drafted reply traces back to the knowledge base, because the expensive failure here isn't rudeness — it's an agent inventing a price or a capability to a real prospect.

The autopilot agent holds no tools

Output guardrails run after tool calls, so an agent holding send_sms can deliver an ungrounded message before any check sees it — and a text can't be unsent. So the agent returns structured output and code decides whether to send. The same reasoning drives retrieval: the knowledge base is lexical rather than vector, because an off-topic question must retrieve nothing, and similarity search always returns a nearest neighbour.

Rules that must always hold live in code, not prompts

Five rules migrated out of prompts during development: the CASL opt-out footer, GSM-7 punctuation, the sub-4.0 ratings the hook agent kept building insulting angles from, and two others. An instruction followed 98% of the time isn't a guarantee, and twice, tightening the instruction caused the next failure instead of fixing it. The compliance tests assert the paths where a bug is a legal problem — a suppressed number can never be sent to, opt-outs survive prospect deletion, a blocked message never returns silence.

Where it stands

Deployed on Railway and reachable on a public demo line; onboarding for paying trades is in progress, so what I can report is latency and booking accuracy rather than revenue. I'd rather show what I've measured than what I hope for.

The two fields that have to be exactly right

A booking is worthless if the street address or callback number is wrong by one digit, and those are precisely the fields ASR mangles — proper nouns and digit strings have no language-model prior to fall back on. Both are read back to the caller for confirmation before the calendar write, and the structured payload is validated before it reaches the booking tool. Everything else in the conversation can tolerate a transcription error; these two cannot.

Why Vapi, and where I went underneath it

Reimplementing a managed voice runtime is a three-week infrastructure project that produces something worse. The real risk of building on a provider isn't the provider — it's that the abstraction has a bottom an interviewer reaches in eight minutes. So one route runs on Pipecat with my own endpointing and barge-in handling, which is where the interesting decisions actually live: how long a pause means "your turn" varies by caller, and getting it wrong either talks over people or leaves dead air.

How it works

  1. Customer calls the business's existing number.
  2. Call forwards to the agent when the line is busy, on a job, or after hours.
  3. Agent collects and confirms name, phone, address, and the nature of the job.
  4. Checks the real Google Calendar and books an open slot live on the call.
  5. Owner and customer both get a text confirmation.

Agentic Task Runner

Built to answer one question: does a hand-rolled planner/executor beat the SDK's native tool-calling loop? It doesn't, and the write-up says why.

FastAPI OpenAI Agents SDK tool_choice=required
Repository →

The decision that changed the design

I started with a custom planner/executor, then switched to native @function_tool with tool_choice="required". The reason is specific: in the custom version each tool call was planned before prior results existed, whereas the SDK's conversation loop puts real results in context before the next call is chosen. Planning ahead of your own outputs is planning blind.

The failure that needed an extra tool

Forced tool use means the model must pick something, so on trivial inputs it reached for whichever tool was nearest and produced nonsense. Adding DirectAnswerTool as an explicit "no tool applies" option fixed it — the constraint needed an escape hatch, not a better prompt.

DeepAnalytic — RAG over the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy

A retrieval pipeline I built, then measured until it contradicted me twice: the reranker was making answers worse, and the section-aware chunking the whole design rests on didn't beat a far simpler splitter.

What it is

A retrieval-augmented QA system over the full 2024 Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, scraped myself. The corpus is a deliberately hard case: dense, heavily cross-referenced, and full of terms whose meaning shifts between sub-fields. The pipeline chunks each article along its own table-of-contents boundaries, embeds to Pinecone, retrieves, reranks with Cohere, and generates — with a naive-retrieval path kept alongside so every change can be measured against a baseline rather than assumed to help.

I was wrong about chunking, and the test said so

The premise was that chunking along real section boundaries would beat fixed token windows, since a blind splitter can drop two unrelated subsections into one chunk. A five-way comparison — section-aware, fixed-window, paragraph, sentence-window, semantic — says otherwise. On straightforward questions, section-aware, fixed-window and paragraph chunking retrieved near-identical text, word for word in places, because paragraph breaks in a well-edited article already track topic breaks closely enough that a structure-blind splitter lands in the same place.

Semantic chunking was the interesting failure: the only strategy that ever clearly won a question, and the only one that ever clearly lost one — it caught a specific example nothing else found, then drifted into an unrelated passage about heresy when asked about universals. That volatility is the finding. I'm not putting retrieval quality on a coin flip, so it's disqualified as a default despite the ceiling. Section-aware stays, not because it won but because nothing beat it and it matches the source structure.

The bottleneck is after retrieval, not before it

On composite questions — compare something in one part of an article to something in another — nearly every strategy failed the same way: it retrieved both halves of the evidence and then declined to synthesize them, reporting that the context provided no direct comparison. The material was in the window. That's a generation failure, and no amount of re-cutting the source text addresses it.

The systematic reranker eval pointed at the same place from a different direction. Reranking came out flat-to-worse across the board, with failures reproducible against the retrieved chunk text. On organic versus somatic animalism, the reranked context kept a chunk that names both terms and points at where they're explained — an explicit backward reference to section 1.2 — while discarding the two chunks section 1.2 was pointing at. On Cicero's res publica, retrieval kept the right chunk and the model still refused, because the causal claim was assembled across the surrounding section rather than stated in one place. A third case was harder still: a chunk opening "the third negative claim Oruka aimed to challenge," with no explicit link to follow and the section listing all three never retrieved at all.

One root cause, three shapes: a chunk can mention or presuppose content it doesn't contain, and nothing in the pipeline notices the gap. Explicit pointers are followable if you look for cross-references. Implicit ones — an ordinal, a presupposition, a causal link spread across a section — need the model to recognize it's filling a gap rather than reporting, which is a different fix.

How the evaluation was built

Five articles chosen for known interesting cases rather than coverage — two confirmed rerank failures, one accuracy-drift case, one cross-article retrieval case, one clean baseline — two questions each, run through both retrieval modes and scored on Accuracy, Source Match and Completeness against expected key points fixed before any answers existed.

Methodology follows current RAG-eval practice: LLM-assisted test-set generation paired with LLM-as-judge scoring and human verification. Claude generated the article selection, questions and rubric, built the automation script, and did first-pass scoring; I scored independently afterward, spot-checking the highest-stakes rows against the retrieved chunk text directly. Both score sets sit side by side in tests/eval_scored.xlsx, including a row where my pass corrected Claude's.

Corpus, and what's next

The full 2024 Stanford Encyclopedia is scraped — article text, tables of contents, bibliographies, metadata. Metadata for 28 Springer Nature philosophy journals (~800k pages) and ~10k pages from Analysis are scraped but not yet indexed. Retrieval currently runs on a 100-article slice.

Next is the generation layer rather than more retrieval tuning, because that's where the evidence points: a cheap prompt test first, to rule out how much of the refusal behaviour is prompt rather than architecture, then multiquery decomposition and a self-critique step, then multi-hop retrieval that follows a pointer inside a chunk to the section that actually explains the concept. Full-corpus indexing, a FastAPI service and deployment after that. Philosophers get it before any of that lands — their queries will be a better adversarial set than anything I'd write for myself.

Structured Propositions & Logics of Ground

Peer-reviewed paper in Synthese providing a unified semantics for propositional logics of impure ground using structured propositions.

Read paper →

Abstract

I show that the assumption of highly structured propositions can be leveraged to provide a unified semantics for various propositional logics of impure ground in a very expressive and flexible way. It is shown, in particular, that the induced models are capable of capturing an infinitude of grounding facts that follow from unrestricted logics of ground, but, due to certain artificial restrictions, are left unaccounted for by the existing semantics in the literature. It is also shown that our models, unlike the ones in the literature, are easily extendable to capture certain distinct views about iterated as well as identity grounding.
